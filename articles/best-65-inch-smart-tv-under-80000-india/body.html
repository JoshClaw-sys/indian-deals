What actually matters in a 65-inch TV under ₹80,000

Something real changed in this bracket during 2026. A year ago, ₹80,000 bought a competent 65-inch edge-lit LED panel and nothing more interesting, with Mini LED reserved for ₹1.2 lakh and up. That is no longer true: Hisense and TCL both ship genuine dimming-zone Mini LED panels at 65 inches inside this budget, so the meaningful choice is no longer which brand but which backlight technology you are actually paying for.

That matters because "QLED" has become close to meaningless as a shopping signal. QLED describes a quantum-dot colour film layered over a backlight; it says nothing about how that backlight is controlled. An edge-lit panel with a quantum-dot film is legally a QLED and will still show grey-ish blacks and visible halos.

If you only buy one thing from this list: the Hisense 65E8S. It is the cheapest 65-inch panel in India with a real Mini LED backlight, roughly 700 nits peak brightness, 144Hz with VRR for consoles, and Dolby Vision plus Dolby Atmos. Confirm the E8S suffix on the listing, because the E7 and A7 models sit at overlapping prices with materially weaker panels.

Backlight: Mini LED, full-array, or edge-lit

This is the single spec that determines how a 65-inch panel looks in a real room. Edge-lit panels put LEDs along one or two edges and diffuse light across the screen, giving perhaps two to sixteen controllable zones. Full-array panels use a grid behind the screen, typically 20 to 60 zones. Mini LED shrinks those LEDs so the same area holds hundreds of zones.

The practical difference shows up in two places. Watching a night-time cricket match, an edge-lit panel washes the stadium a uniform dark grey. On credits or subtitles over black, edge-lit panels bloom a visible glow around the text. At 65 inches both artefacts are larger and harder to ignore than on a 43-inch set.

Brightness: insist on a nits figure

Peak brightness in nits is the most commonly hidden spec in Indian TV listings, and its absence is informative. Budget 65-inch panels frequently sit at 250 to 350 nits. Mini LED models in this bracket reach roughly 600 to 700 nits. HDR content is mastered assuming displays can hit at least 600 nits, so on a 300-nit panel the HDR version of a film can genuinely look flatter than the SDR version.

Brightness also decides whether the TV is watchable in daylight. A 300-nit panel facing a window at 4pm is close to unusable; a 700-nit panel stays legible. If a listing advertises HDR without publishing a nits figure, assume it is under 350.

Refresh rate: 120Hz and 144Hz are now reachable

All five picks here are 4K, so resolution is not a differentiator at this price. Refresh rate is. A 60Hz panel is fine for films and broadcast content, which is mastered at 24 or 50 frames per second anyway. A 120Hz or 144Hz panel matters if you own a PS5, an Xbox Series X or a gaming PC, and it helps fast-panning sports look less smeary.

The related spec is VRR, or variable refresh rate, which lets the panel match the console's fluctuating output frame by frame and eliminates tearing. The Hisense E8S and TCL Q6C support both high refresh and VRR; the Sony, Samsung and LG picks are 60Hz.

Sound: assume you will add a soundbar

Every panel in this bracket ships with 20W to 40W of audio inside a chassis roughly 30mm deep, and physics does not permit meaningful bass from that enclosure. Dolby Atmos in the specification list means the TV can decode an Atmos stream, not that you will hear height channels. Plan on ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 for a 2.1 soundbar with a wireless subwoofer, and check for eARC on one HDMI port.

Warranty: read the split, not the headline

Indian TV warranties are almost always split into comprehensive cover and panel-only cover. A listing advertising a three-year warranty usually means one year comprehensive plus two years panel-only, which puts labour, mainboard, power supply and backlight driver out of scope after year one. Since backlight drivers commonly fail around year three, that split matters more than the headline number. Sony, Samsung and LG have the deepest 65-inch service coverage in tier-2 cities; Hisense and TCL remain metro-strongest.

Frequently asked questions

Is Mini LED worth the extra money over QLED at 65 inches under ₹80,000?

Yes, if you watch in a room with any ambient light. Mini LED uses hundreds of small backlight zones that can dim independently, so bright objects stay bright while surrounding areas go dark. A QLED-labelled edge-lit panel has a handful of zones along one edge, which produces visible halos around bright objects on dark backgrounds. The Hisense E8S and TCL Q6C are the two picks here with genuine dimming-zone backlights.

Is 65 inches too big for a small Indian living room?

The practical rule for 4K is a viewing distance of roughly 1.2 to 1.5 times the screen diagonal, which puts a 65-inch panel at about 6.5 to 8 feet. Most Indian living rooms seat viewers 8 to 11 feet from the TV wall, so 65 inches is comfortable. Below 6 feet you will start noticing pixel structure on lower-bitrate streams and cable feeds.

Do I need HDMI 2.1 if I only stream?

No. Streaming apps run on the TV itself and never touch an HDMI port. HDMI 2.1 with 4K 120Hz matters for a PS5, Xbox Series X or a gaming PC. If you only use Netflix, Prime Video and JioHotstar, prioritise panel brightness and Dolby Vision support instead and treat port specs as secondary.

Why do brand TVs cost double the unbranded 65-inch listings?

Three reasons. First, panel grade: budget listings frequently use B-grade panels with weaker uniformity and lower brightness. Second, processing: upscaling a 1080p cable feed to 4K is a software problem, and brand processors are years ahead. Third, service: brand networks stock 65-inch replacement panels regionally, while an unbranded panel failure at month 14 often has no repair path at all.

How long will these TVs keep receiving app updates?

Google TV models such as the TCL Q6C and Sony K-65S30B generally get platform updates for around four years. Samsung Tizen and LG webOS keep major streaming apps working for a similar span. Custom Android builds on unbranded panels are the risk case: apps commonly stop updating within 12 to 18 months and Netflix or JioHotstar can drop certification entirely.

The bottom line

The headline change in this bracket is that Mini LED is no longer a premium-only technology at 65 inches. If picture quality per rupee is the goal, the Hisense 65E8S is the pick: real dimming zones, roughly 700 nits, 144Hz with VRR, and both major HDR formats, usually under ₹78,000. Accept a smaller app library in exchange.

For that panel technology with mainstream software, the TCL 65Q6C is the same idea on Google TV. If most of your viewing is cable and mixed-quality streaming, the Sony K-65S30B wins on processing despite the weaker backlight. On a tight budget the Samsung UA65DUE70B has the best service reach here, and the LG 65UT80 is the pick when ease of use outranks specification.

Verify the exact model suffix before paying, treat any HDR claim without a nits figure as suspect, and budget ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 for a soundbar.
